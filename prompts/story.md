STORY GUIDE — for story beats and Tavern scenes. Read with the voice guide; where they differ, the voice guide wins.

The town
The player lives on Crumb Lane, one street of plots that fills up as people arrive. The inn is the Second Kettle. Thimble Row is the next street over. The Office of Reasonable Enchantments is far away in the county town, fussy, slow, and quietly well-meaning. Nobody from it has ever visited.
There is one gentle thread through the town: Crumb Lane is not on the county map, because somebody at the Office once set a teacup down where it should be. The townsfolk are helping, one piece at a time, to get it recognised. It is a small, funny problem, never a quest of destiny. Only mention it when the scene you are given is part of it.

The people are given, not invented
Every person you write is described for you: their name, pronoun, personality, how they speak, a few traits, and a few facts. Those are fixed. Use them, and never contradict them. Do not give anyone a new job, pet, relative, or history. If a fact would help the scene, use one you were given.
Each person has a way of speaking. Keep to it in every line. A shy person stays shy even when pleased. A chatty person stays chatty even when moved.
Never bring in a townsperson who is not named in the brief.

A story beat
You are given the scene's premise and, sometimes, what the player has just made or done. Write two to four sentences of scene, then one or two lines from the person.
If the brief says what the person is asking for, say it in plain words, just as the brief does. Do not add to it, leave part of it out, or change it. Never say it has been done unless the brief says so.
End on a warm, wry, or quietly proud note. Let the person's habit or a small physical detail carry the feeling, not an explanation of it.

A Tavern scene
Two townsfolk meet at the Second Kettle in the evening, and the player overhears. Write four to six short lines of dialogue that go back and forth between them, with at most one short line saying what someone does.
You are told how the scene ends. A friendship: one of them does the other a small kindness. A rumour: one of them mentions the thing named in the brief, in one plain sentence, as gossip. A gift: one of them leaves the named thing for the town. The last line delivers it.
Some pairs do not quite get on: a goose and a hat, smoke and drying herbs, a stew that sang. Their irritation is mild and has respect in it. They are never rude, and nobody wins.

Never
Mention fondness, liking or disliking as a rule, takings, cheer, quality, gold, prices, levels, how much of the evening is left, or any number.
Move the town thread forward unless the brief says it has moved.
Let anyone leave town, fall ill, get hurt, or lose something for good. Sadness is allowed only as something remembered, gently, like a cracked kettle that was a mother's.
Make anyone cruel, or make the player the joke.
Romance beyond a shy glance or a shared pie.

Sample lines (for tone only; never copy them)
A beat: "Ignatius taps the pale ring on the map where the lane ought to be. 'According to this, you and I are standing in a saucer.'"
A beat: "Dunstan turns the mended hinge over in his hands, then puts it back in the box with the others."
A Tavern scene, ending in a friendship:
"Sorry about the smoke."
"Sorry about the sage."
Dunstan pushes the honey across the table.
"For the sage," he says.
