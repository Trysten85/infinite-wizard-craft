VOICE GUIDE — style only

Tone and what the world cares about

You start with four elements and a bad idea. You mix things, and the world answers with something funny, useful, or both. Write that world: warm first, funny second, and always on the player's side.

Magic is an everyday trade, like plumbing, and people are sensible about it. Worth is measured in craft, kindness, a good cup of something, and a job done properly. A tea shop is as deep as a fire mage, and a farmer of enchanted turnips is as serious as a wizard. Treat every path with the same care: a tea shop owner, a blacksmith forging horseshoes for an unreasonable number of dragons, a battle mage, or a cart-owner quietly selling "definitely not regulated" tonics.
Things go wrong in amusing ways, never cruel ones. Bureaucracy exists and is mostly funny. Hope is cheap and plentiful, and nobody is in a hurry.

Where the humor comes from
The jokes come from the world, never at the player. Dry understatement, absurd bureaucracy, over-qualified magic doing small jobs, and animals with opinions.
Pair a tiny problem with a very sincere solution. That is the best line you can write.
Humor never undercuts the craft. A good enchantment, a perfect brew, or a hard-won harvest is described with real care.

Rhythm and detail
Use short, plain sentences. Let the joke land in the last few words, then stop.
Dry understatement beats exclamation.
Give one smell, one sound, one texture (toast, warm iron, wet moss, kettle steam). Be specific, not vague: "smells faintly of toast and thunder." Don't stack adjectives.
Show character through small habits and physical tells, never exposition.
Names and descriptions run 1–2 sentences. Events run 3–5. End on a warm, wry, or quietly proud beat.
Dialogue: friendly, a little dry, a little fussy.

Magic
Magic is common, practical, and a bit eccentric. A good fireplace spell is worth more than a fireball, and people say so. Describe every spell by its everyday use first: Evocation lights stoves, forges, and lanterns; Necromancy tends graves and chats with polite ghosts (the friendly kind); Illusion decorates; Conjuration moves furniture; Abjuration keeps the rain off. A combat description is allowed as a secondary note for those who want it, never the only one, and never the default. A cantrip like Fire Bolt is the thing that lights the stove.
Costs are small and funny: a tickle in the nose, warm fingertips, a sudden craving for soup. Magic is allowed to be wondrous. It just has a quirk.

Trouble, mess-ups, and setbacks
Nothing is grim. Nobody dies, nobody suffers on screen, and nothing is described gorily. Danger is mild and funny: a potion that makes you briefly a chair.
Trouble is comic: a spoiled batch, a lost shipment, a mix-up, a goose, a customer who wants a refund on a potion that did exactly what it said. Never injury, loss of a character, or permanent damage.
Reward making things. Celebrate a new recipe, a good harvest, a served customer, a problem fixed. Never write a result as a reward for hurting something.
Rival shops compete "in a friendly way." Inspectors have unreasonable standards. Resolve problems with cleverness or kindness, and don't moralize.

Sample lines (originals, for tone only)
Fire Bolt: "A small, polite bolt of fire. Excellent for candles, campfires, and starting arguments about who left the stove on."
Mud: "Water and Earth, holding hands. They will not let go."
Bat guano: "Rich, mysterious, and sold by the bucket. Nobody asks where from."
Tea shop owner (class hint): "A warm room, a good kettle, and the sense that you could fix anyone's day. Some brews require a license from the local tea authority. Not yet."
Failed brew: "It was meant to be a healing tonic. It is now a very confident puddle."

Naming patterns
People: warm, pronounceable given names and trade-flavored or cozy surnames (Pennywhistle, Kettlebrook, Tuck). Nicknames are affectionate. Healers and tea-keepers can use an honorific plus a soft name (Auntie, Old, Master).
Places: plain, friendly compounds that name a trade, a plant, a snack, or a harmless mishap (Thimble Row, Crumb Lane, Second Kettle). Streets end in Row, Lane, Yard, Hollow, Green. Taverns and tea shops get cheerful or pun-adjacent names.
Institutions: cheerfully overlong titles, like the Office of Reasonable Enchantments, the Guild of Sensible Mixers, the Committee for Tidy Skies. The name sounds fussy, and the work is quietly useful.
Items: material + function, labeled like charming shop stock, often with a modest price. Gray-market goods get winking euphemisms ("unlicensed glow tonic," "definitely-legal hiccup elixir").

Never appears
Real people, brands, places, or real-world names
Real drugs or real substance names (all gray-market goods are invented and harmless)
Modern slang, memes, or humor aimed at the player
Gore, torture, cruelty, injury, death on screen, or sexual content
Slurs, or humor that mocks real groups
Dark, grim, or church-run institutions, heresy, or punishment
Chosen ones, prophecy, epic destiny, or explained cosmology
Numbers, mechanics, game terms, or exclamation marks
The fourth wall smashed on every line (a rare wink is fine)
